What is a dental implant, really?

Dr Pushkar Sobti
All-on-X implantologist, Delhi

When I put a dental implant in a patient's hand for the first time, the reaction is almost always the same: "That's it?" Most people picture something large and mechanical. What they are holding is a small titanium post, usually 3.5 to 5 millimetres wide and 8 to 13 millimetres long. It weighs less than a gram.

That small post does the job of a tooth root. Everything else in an implant tooth is built on top of it.

Three parts, one tooth

A dental implant is not one piece. A complete implant tooth has three parts, and each has a separate job.

  • The implant. A threaded titanium post placed in the jawbone where the root used to be. Once healed, it is the foundation for everything above it.
  • The abutment. The connector that screws into the implant and rises through the gum. At our clinic it is custom-made for each patient, so it matches the shape of the gum and supports the crown at the right angle.
  • The crown. The visible tooth. We design it digitally and mill it from zirconia in our own lab, matched to the shape and shade of your other teeth.

When a full arch of teeth is replaced, as in All-on-4, the idea is the same. Four to six implants carry one fixed bridge instead of separate crowns.

Why titanium bonds to bone

In the 1950s a Swedish researcher, Per-Ingvar Brånemark, was studying blood flow in bone. He placed small titanium chambers in bone, and when he tried to remove them he found he couldn't. The bone had grown tightly onto the titanium surface. He called it osseointegration, and in 1965 he treated his first dental implant patient.

Titanium works because its surface forms a thin, stable oxide layer that the body does not treat as foreign. Bone cells grow directly onto it. Modern implants also have specially treated surfaces that give bone more to grip and help it attach faster.

That bonding takes time. After the implant is placed, the bone usually needs about three to four months to fuse with it fully. In suitable cases we can fit temporary teeth much sooner, which I will explain later in this series.

How an implant differs from a natural tooth

An implant comes close to a natural tooth, but it isn't the same, and patients should know where it differs.

  • No cavities. Titanium and zirconia cannot decay.
  • Gums still need care. The gum and bone around an implant can still become inflamed if plaque builds up. Brushing and regular check-ups matter as much as they did for your natural teeth.
  • A slightly different feel. A natural tooth sits in a cushion of ligament fibres. An implant is fused directly to bone, so it feels firm and solid. Most patients stop noticing the difference within weeks.

The implants I use

Not all implants are the same. The design of the thread, the surface treatment and the connection to the abutment all affect how well an implant holds and how long it lasts. In my practice I use three systems: Nobel Biocare NobelActive TiUltra, Hiossen ET IV and Osstem TS IV CA. I choose between them for each patient based on the bone quality seen on the CBCT scan and the type of restoration planned.

Questions

  • How big is a dental implant?

    Most implants are about 3.5 to 5 mm wide and 8 to 13 mm long. The exact size is chosen for each patient from the CBCT scan, based on how much bone there is.

  • Is a dental implant a screw?

    The implant itself is a small threaded titanium post that sits in the jawbone like a root. On top of it go an abutment, which connects, and a crown, which is the tooth you see.

  • Can you be allergic to a titanium implant?

    True titanium allergy is very rare. For patients who want a metal-free option, zirconia (ceramic) implants exist, and I cover them later in this series.
